Tyndale’s Words, 500 Years On
Eight familiar phrases, each shown two ways: before Tyndale — the Latin Vulgate and John Wycliffe’s 1395 English, translated from Latin, not the original tongues — and after, in Tyndale’s own 1520s–30s translation, much of it still read today.

Genesis 1:3  ·  Before Tyndale
Vulgate, Latin
Dixitque Deus: Fiat lux. Et facta est lux.
“And God said: Let there be made light. And light was made.”
Wycliffe, 1395
And God seide, Liȇt be maad, and liȇt was maad.

Genesis 4:9  ·  Before Tyndale
Vulgate, Latin
Et ait Dominus ad Cain: Ubi est Abel frater tuus? Qui respondit: Nescio, num custos fratris mei sum.
“And the Lord said to Cain: Where is thy brother Abel? Who answered: I know not, am I the keeper of my brother?”
Wycliffe, 1395
And the Lord said to Cain, Where is Abel thy brother? Which answered, I know not; whether I am the keeper of my brother?

Genesis 4:9  ·  Tyndale, 1520s–30s
And the LORde sayd vnto Cain: where is Abell thy brother? And he sayd: I can not tell am I my brothers keper?

“Am I my brother’s keeper?”
Still one of the most-quoted lines in the English language, essentially unchanged since Tyndale.
John 1:1  ·  Before Tyndale
Vulgate, Latin

Wycliffe, 1395
In the beginning was the word, and the word was at God, and God was the word.

John 1:1  ·  Tyndale, 1520s–30s
In the beginnynge was the worde and the worde was with God: and the worde was God.

“In the beginning was the Word”
Wycliffe’s “at God” becomes Tyndale’s “with God” — the King James Version, and every modern Bible since, follows Tyndale.
Romans 13:1–2  ·  Before Tyndale
Vulgate, Latin
Omnis anima potestatibus sublimioribus subdita sit… quae autem sunt a Deo, ordinatae sunt.
“Let every soul be subject to the higher powers… and those that are of God, are ordained.”
Wycliffe, 1395
Every soul be subject to higher powers. For there is no power but of God, and those things that be of God, be ordained.

Romans 13:1–2  ·  Tyndale, 1520s–30s

“The powers that be”
This exact phrase passed unchanged into the King James Bible and is still everyday English, five hundred years later.

“A stranger in a strange land”
Tyndale’s echo between “stranger” and “strange” became a permanent piece of English idiom — even the title of a famous 1961 science-fiction novel.

“Let my people go”
Tyndale’s direct, commanding phrase later became a rallying cry echoed in spirituals, civil rights oratory, and protest movements for centuries after.

Matthew 27:50  ·  Before Tyndale
Vulgate, Latin

“…sent forth his spirit.”
Wycliffe, 1395

Matthew 27:50  ·  Tyndale, 1520s–30s

← Turn back
“He gave up the ghost”
A rare case where Wycliffe’s wording stuck, not Tyndale’s own “yelded vp the goost” — the KJV translators kept the older phrase. Tyndale didn’t erase everything before him.
